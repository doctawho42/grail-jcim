Introduction

A drug that reaches the bloodstream does not
remain the compound that was administered.
Enzymes, predominantly hepatic, convert it into
other compounds, and those compounds carry
activity, toxicity and persistence of their own.
Some of them are the species that acts: the
antiviral prodrugs are inactive as administered
and reach their target only after phosphorylation 1 . Others end a development programme,
as the reactive intermediate implicated in the
withdrawal of troglitazone did 2 . Knowing which
compounds an enzyme system will make from a
candidate drug therefore belongs with the properties a medicinal chemist asks about early.
Experimental metabolite identification answers the question for a compound that has
been synthesised and dosed. A computational
prediction is available earlier, while a compound
exists only as a drawing, and that is when its
structure can still be changed in response. The
prediction asked for here is the chemical structure of each metabolite, which is a harder object
than the quantities usually predicted alongside
it. A clearance rate is a number and a site of
metabolism is an atom, whereas a metabolite is

arguing that a difference should be shown practically significant and prescribing how such a
comparison ought to be constructed. Özçelik
and Grisoni 18 show what follows when it is not,
in generative molecular design, where the size of
the library and the choice of metric reorder the
models. The multiverse and specification-curve
literature 19,20 reports results across the whole
space of defensible analysis choices, and the
present work applies that discipline to metabolite structure prediction. A protocol tells an
author what to declare. Generative metrics are
distributional, and the axis that reorders methods here, the number of candidates a system
may emit, belongs to ranked output alone, so
recall against an incomplete annotation needs
its own demonstration.
We report GRAIL (Graph-scored Rule Application with Inspectable Localisation), a predictor of metabolite structures assembled from
a bank of reaction templates, a learned model
that selects which templates to apply to a given
substrate, and a learned model that ranks the
structures those templates produce. GRAIL is
the engine of MetaTox 3.0 at way2drug.com,
replacing MetaTox 7 as the predictor that service answers with; Section 3 compares the two.
We evaluate GRAIL with both of the decisions
above raised to declared axes, each named and
each swept across its full range, against seven
published predictors we obtained and ran ourselves on one common set of drug-like substrates.
This paper leaves three things behind it. The
first is the comparison as data: the per-substrate
predictions of all nine arms on the 1,170 substrates, frozen and released with the harness that
scores them, so any cell of any table here can be
recomputed and any later system added to the
same grid under the same two axes. For want
of such a set, the orderings in this literature are
at present checkable only by their authors. The
second is a bound: one application of the whole
bank reaches 0.8171 of known metabolites in a
single step, and a census of what it misses says
which chemistry is absent and where to look for
it, which holds of every bank mined from the
sources this field trains on. The third is what

Two families of method are in use. Rule-based
systems encode known biotransformations as
reaction templates, patterns that match a substructure of the substrate and rewrite it, and
they enumerate the products of applying those
templates. SyGMa 3 , BioTransformer 4 , GLORY
and GLORYx 5,6 and MetaTox 7 are of this kind.
Sequence-to-sequence systems treat the problem
as translation between string representations of
the substrate and of its metabolites, and MetaTrans 8 and MetaPredictor 9 are of this kind.
Both lines have been extended recently 10–13 .
The two families are used for different reasons.
A template names the transformation it applied
and the atoms it acted on, so a chemist can
see on what grounds a structure was proposed
and can reject it mechanistically; that property
is what makes rule-based systems usable in a
discovery workflow. A sequence model reports
the structure alone, and is valued for reaching
chemistry that lies outside any template bank.
Choosing between the two families, or between
systems within one of them, requires a comparison that the published literature does not
presently support. The usual measure is recall:
of the metabolites recorded for a compound, the
fraction that a system returns. That fraction depends on two decisions taken outside the model.
The first is when a returned structure counts
as one of the recorded metabolites, since two
structures can denote the same molecule while
differing in the placement of a hydrogen or the
drawing of a ring. The second is how many
structures a system is permitted to return, since
any system recovers more of the answer when
allowed a longer list. Published predictors differ
on both decisions and report them inconsistently.
The systems compared here return between 10.6
and 80.7 structures per substrate under their
default settings, so their recall figures sit on different scales. Independent evaluations of these
tools have encountered the same obstacle 14–16 .
That a comparison can turn on an undeclared
choice has been argued before, and two recent
statements of it bound what the present work

Materials and methods

2.1

Corpus, splits and evaluation
populations

The predictor

GRAIL works in three stages, and each can be
inspected on its own. A generator scores every rule in a bank of reaction templates against
the substrate and selects which of them to apply. The selected rules are then applied with
RDKit, and their products are standardised 25,26
and deduplicated. A rule that breaks a bond
yields more than one product, and every product and every disconnected component of one
enters separately provided it retains at least
two heavy atoms, which discards a lone leaving
group while keeping a small metabolite such
as formate. A surviving product is a candidate, and the candidates generated for one substrate are its pool. Finally a scorer evaluates
each substrate–candidate pair, encoding the two
molecules in separate molecular graphs, and the
pool is ordered by a fusion of the scorer’s ranking
with the generator’s.
The bank holds 7,581 templates spanning
4,417 reaction types, where a type is the multiset
of bonds that change between mapped atoms. A
template is a mapped SMIRKS pattern, and we
use rule for the same object here and in the released files. The bank is the deduplicated union
of 1,233 templates from three curated collections, 5,856 mined from the training split alone,
and 492 that an earlier machine extraction took
from the same annotated pairs. At least 966
of the 1,233 curated templates, 78.3%, appear
verbatim in rule sets published by others, so
this work’s contribution is the mining, the ranking and the measurement. Support across the
mined templates is heavily skewed, with 79.5%
of them resting on a single training pair, and
a rarefaction curve over withheld pairs is still
climbing, so this corpus still yields templates
that are new.

can reach the same candidate, and such a candidate receives a single score. Writing R(c) for

Every interval reported here is a percomparison interval with no family-wise adjustment, and we report the corrected reading
beside it. Holm’s correction over the family
declared in advance, the two arms against three
comparators, leaves 22 of the 33 separating cells

wide-budget result and every conclusion drawn

is made for a cell only where that cell’s interval
excludes zero, and where an ordering is not
established the table says so.

Whether a returned structure counts as one of
the known metabolites is settled by the matching criterion, which we treat as a declared axis
with five settings and report under all five. The
tautomer-aware key is the default, because the
known metabolites are recorded as constitutions with tautomers merged, and because standard identifiers normalise only a subset of tautomers 28 . Recording constitutions alone also
bounds what these systems can be asked for:
this pipeline works at the level of constitution
throughout, so sulfoxidation, epoxide hydrolysis and most conjugation are predicted with
configuration left open.
A third choice sits behind both declared axes:
how the substrate itself is drawn. Templates
are matched against the structure the corpus
stores, and that corpus draws amides as imidic
acids, so the choice is not neutral. Rebuilding
both GRAIL arms with every substrate redrawn
moves 1 of 54 arm-against-comparator verdicts,
none of them a result claimed here.

Agreement between the reported site and the observed
change

Every prediction names the transformation rule
that produced it and the site at which it fired, so
every candidate carries the mechanistic grounds
on which a reader can reject it. Site predic10

tion is a task with its own literature and its
own tools, reactivity-based 29 and learned 30,31 ,
resting on annotation curated for the purpose 32
whose derivation that literature has recently had
to confront 33 . Our claim is the narrower one
that the site this system names is the site that
changed.
We measure that consistency on the interactive arm. Of 327 correctly predicted metabolites,
the reported site touches the atoms that differ
from the substrate in 92.3% and lies wholly inside the change in 46.2% (Section S22). The
rate on its own overstates the effect, since a
site chosen without regard to the chemistry will
sometimes overlap the change by accident; measured against a connected null fragment of the
same size, the margin is 0.5715.
Two things bound the reading. The reference centre is derived by the routine that located the reaction centre when a template was
mined, so for a mined template the two are
not independent and the agreement is partly a
self-consistency check. Split by provenance, the
hand-written templates agree at 91.1% against
93.1% for the mined ones, so the claim rests on
the half whose provenance guarantees nothing.
The audit runs on the interactive arm alone at
a rule budget of 30, and the whole bank would
score a different population of matches. What
a chemist gets from an attribution also depends
on the template: 10.8% of parseable templates
have three reactant atoms or fewer, and there
the learned stages supply the discrimination between sites.

4.5

Scope of the coverage bound

The shortfall is a property of the corpus the
templates are mined from, so extending coverage requires new reaction corpora, and the
census narrows the search. Synthetic corpora
are excluded by measurement, which leaves enzymatic and regulatory sources. The regulatory ones are the interesting half: metabolite
identifications from radiolabelled human ADME
studies are human in vivo data on exactly the
population predicted here, they have been used
to benchmark this class of tool 16 , and mining
them systematically as a source of rules is, to
our knowledge, still open. Whether the same
bound holds for a sequence model, which works
without a template bank, is an open question.
What the bound licenses has to be said plainly,
because the corpus it is a property of has two
defects this paper also reports: its assembly cannot be reconstructed, and the one source held on
disk shows the selection was made with chemistry in view. The bound therefore says one
thing: mining the training split harder leaves
the shortfall where it is, because that chemistry
is absent from it. Its scope stops there. Stating
what fraction of human metabolism lies in types
every corpus omits would need a corpus whose
assembly is recoverable and whose selection is
documented. The narrower statement still earns
its place, because this corpus is assembled from
the four sources this field trains on, so a bank

Limitations

as one enzymatic step. Applied to the templates, the registered instrument flags 15.4%
of the mined ones it can score as composite,
clearing the bar registered beforehand. A second instrument flags a smaller share and errs

so its figure is an estimate of unknown sign
(Section S23). The ceiling is therefore a onetemplate one where a single enzymatic step may
take several, and that share is why a second
application buys little: a depth-two probe put
the lift at 0.012 at 8.5 times the cost.
Two registered mechanisms failed: emitting
candidates in whole groups, and gating on the
group signal before the fusion. An oracle closes
the second, since even a perfect ordering through
it is worse than the generator cap it replaces
(Section S26).
Both learned stages are trained short of what
the split allows, on a subsample, and the runs
ended on their epoch budget with early stopping
unused, an underfitting signature; retraining on
the full split is left to future work (Section S31).
One checkpoint produced every figure, so the
intervals quantify the population with training
held fixed. Against the noise three seeds measure, the rank fusion and the pool cap stand
well clear of it and the rule budget sits inside it,
which makes that one indicative only.
The register is weaker than its count suggests.
Two of the six confirmed predictions clear the
falsification condition only as written, comparing a point estimate to a threshold where the
stricter reading asks the whole interval to clear
it: the rank fusion and the pool cap have their
thresholds inside their own intervals, so the data
establish only that the effect exceeds zero. The
group score as a third ranking is weaker again,
its threshold fixed in advance while its population was chosen afterwards, on the comparison
set, derived from the test split and recorded after
the fact; Table 2 records that as a measurement,

Depth, too, was met differently by each arm.
It is swept for SyGMa and BioTransformer,
whose depth is ours to turn, and the wide-budget
results survive both sweeps; the knob stops short
of the output budget.
Nothing in this pipeline is stereochemistryaware. The standardiser strips configuration,
the criterion ignores it and the annotation carries none, so sulfoxidation, epoxide hydrolysis
and most conjugation are scored as constitutions.
That is a restriction on what may be asked of
a metabolite-structure predictor: sulfoxide and
epoxide configuration carry the pharmacology
of several drug classes, and a system working
at the level of constitution can be asked which
structure an enzyme makes but not which enantiomer. What it costs is left open: 36.1% of
annotated pairs gain a stereogenic element new
to the substrate, and the criterion sweep cannot
price a configuration-aware criterion, because
every known metabolite here is recorded without
one (Section S13).
No measurement of a system here is external.
Every figure is measured on one corpus whose
only published reference set is one of its four
sources, so a held-out split of it tests the ranking while the chemistry it is measured on comes
from the same four sources. The selection that
drew those records is lost, and the apparatus
built on top, which makes every step from the
corpus onwards reproducible, leaves that one
step outside it. What can be checked is a rebuild, which the repository reports substrate by
substrate, and the one source held here in full
says the selection was made with chemistry in
view: the types it kept and those it dropped
are differently distributed (Section S18). The
radiolabelled human ADME sets of Section 4.2
would serve, and measuring a system here on one
would need machine-readable structures, where
that benchmark publishes its known metabolites as drawings. Until then the comparison
establishes an ordering within a corpus, which
is weaker than a ranking of the methods.

Conclusions

GRAIL predicts metabolite structures by selecting templates from a bank of reaction rules,
applying them, and ranking the products by
a reciprocal rank fusion of two learned scores.
How those two scores are combined mattered
more than what produced them: replacing their
product with the rank fusion gained more recall than any architectural change tested here.
One application of that bank reaches 0.8171 of
known metabolites in a single step. Most of what
remains is chemistry absent from the training
corpus, so enlarging the bank from the same corpus would leave it there, and every bank mined
from the sources this field trains on inherits that
gap.
Which predictor leads depends on how a candidate is judged to match a known metabolite
and on how many candidates it may return, and
papers in this area declare neither. That is the
case for releasing the grid, and the predictions
it is computed from. The next system can be
added to it, and this one can be checked against
it.
